      Trainer
      The board and the pieces

      Chess is played on an 8×8 board of light and dark squares. White starts at the bottom, and each side has eight pawns, two knights, two bishops, two rooks, a queen and a king. White always moves first.

      Pawns move one square forward (two on their first move) and capture diagonally. Knights jump in an L (two squares one way, one square across). Bishops slide diagonally. Rooks slide in straight lines. The queen does both. The king moves one square in any direction.

      Check, checkmate and stalemate

      A king is in check when an enemy piece attacks its square. You must answer it: move the king, block the attack, or capture the attacker. You may never leave your own king in check.

      Checkmate ends the game — the king is attacked and nothing legal can save it. Stalemate is different: the side to move is not in check but has no legal move, and the game is a draw.

      Castling and en passant

      Castling moves your king two squares toward a rook and puts the rook on the far side of the king. It needs an unmoved king and rook, empty squares between them, and no check on the king’s start, pass-through or destination square.

      En passant: if an enemy pawn advances two squares and lands beside your pawn, you may capture it as though it had moved only one square — but only on the very next move.

      Opening principles

      Fight for the centre, develop your knights and bishops early, castle within the first ten moves, and don’t move the same piece twice without a reason. Bring the queen out late, and only where she is safe.

      Before every move, ask what your opponent’s last move threatened. Most lost games start with an unanswered threat.

      Tactics worth spotting

      Fork: one piece attacks two targets at once. Pin: a piece cannot move because something more valuable sits behind it. Skewer: the valuable piece is in front and must move, exposing what is behind.

      Discovered attack: moving one piece uncovers the attack of another. Back-rank mate: a king trapped behind its own pawns. Checks and captures first — they are how tactics start.

      Endgame basics

      Activate your king in the endgame — it becomes a strong piece once the queens are gone. Passed pawns should be pushed, and rooks belong behind passed pawns.

      King and queen versus king, and king and rook versus king, are routine wins: force the enemy king to the edge, then deliver mate. Learn the opposition in king-and-pawn endings before you need it.
